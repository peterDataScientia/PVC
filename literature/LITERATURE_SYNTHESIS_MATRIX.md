# Thermal Degradation Kinetics and Lifetime Prediction of Perovskite Solar Cells

**Focused literature synthesis and research gap**  
**Date:** 15 November 2026  
**Basis:** Selected primary experimental and computational studies relevant to degradation kinetics, accelerated thermal ageing and T80 prediction. This is a focused critical synthesis, not a systematic review.

## Critical literature synthesis

The operational stability of perovskite solar cells has progressed from a secondary device-performance consideration to a central constraint on photovoltaic deployment. Although high conversion efficiencies demonstrate the potential of these materials, efficiency measured at a single time point cannot establish how a device behaves under prolonged electrical and environmental stress. Jacobsson et al. (2022) addressed the broader problem of fragmented evidence by assembling more than 42,400 published perovskite device records, creating a basis for examining how composition, architecture and fabrication relate to reported performance. This contribution is important for mapping the diversity of available photovoltaic systems, but a large collection of device characteristics is not equivalent to a collection of comparable degradation experiments. Stability measurements differ in duration, illumination, atmosphere, temperature and the definition of the reported endpoint. Consequently, identifying materials associated with high initial efficiency and establishing the kinetics governing their subsequent performance loss are distinct scientific tasks.

Time-resolved studies have begun to address this distinction by examining degradation as an evolving process rather than a single final measurement. Hartono et al. (2023) investigated 2,245 maximum-power-point-tracking (MPPT) ageing trajectories and used statistical analysis and self-organizing maps to distinguish recurring stability patterns over a standardized 150-hour window. Their findings indicated that higher initial device efficiency was associated with better short-term stability in the investigated population, while the trajectories themselves showed substantial heterogeneity. The significance extends beyond identifying stable and unstable devices: different trajectories imply that efficiency loss may include distinct temporal phases, including early changes that are not necessarily representative of later operation. However, grouping curves by observed shape does not determine whether those groups share degradation-rate laws or whether their early behaviour predicts a future lifetime threshold. The relatively short standardized window is therefore well suited to describing early ageing but cannot independently establish long-term operational survival. This separation between **describing an observed curve** and **predicting its future evolution** is essential when evaluating degradation models.

Temperature introduces an additional physical dimension because it changes the timescale over which degradation processes operate. In a thermal-stability study of flexible MAPbI₃ modules, accelerated tests at 85, 95 and 105 °C were analyzed using Arrhenius-type relationships, yielding a reported apparent activation energy of 1.062 eV for degradation of maximum electrical output. The study also described nonuniform or staged behaviour in other electrical indicators. Together, these observations show both the practical value and the limitations of rate-based thermal extrapolation. When a single dominant thermally activated process governs the chosen observable over a consistent regime, an Arrhenius relationship may provide a compact description of temperature sensitivity. When the observable changes through several phases, however, a fitted slope can depend on the ageing interval used to derive the rate. An apparent activation energy extracted from maximum power at 85–105 °C therefore cannot be assumed to represent the same process as an activation energy estimated from MPPT efficiency at 25–65 °C. The latter requires its own examination of kinetic form, time-window dependence and residual behaviour.

The interpretation of temperature-driven ageing also depends on device construction. Research on self-assembled interfacial layers, including the stabilized bilayer architecture reported in *Nature Energy* (2025 volume), has shown that changes in interfacial design can improve thermal stability. Such results connect materials engineering with degradation kinetics: two devices exposed to similar temperatures may exhibit different stability because the structure, interfaces and likely rate-limiting processes differ. They also qualify a purely temperature-based comparison across experimental collections. A difference between measured rates in two archives cannot automatically be attributed to temperature if the devices also differ in architecture, fabrication, measurement conditions or thermal history. The physical question is therefore not simply whether degradation becomes faster with heating, but whether the observed changes can be represented consistently within each experimental system before extrapolation across systems is attempted.

Computational approaches provide another route to quantify relationships between device characteristics and stability, but the predictive objective matters. Alsulami et al. (2024) used 6,038 records for data-driven perovskite stability modeling with sequential minimal optimization regression. Their reported training correlations differed between a p–i–n architecture-specific subset (0.963) and the pooled architecture analysis (0.699). These results suggest that device heterogeneity can influence relationships recovered from the data, although training correlations do not establish prediction accuracy for independent samples. They also demonstrate why comparisons between models built using different device populations are difficult to interpret as evidence of superior algorithms. A favorable fitted statistic may arise from a more homogeneous dataset, a different outcome definition, or the evaluation design itself. Accordingly, rigorous model comparison requires common data partitions, consistent endpoint definitions and separation of related measurements—especially where several pixels originate from the same fabricated sample.

Lifetime prediction adds a further requirement that is not captured by accurate reproduction of a measured efficiency trajectory. Dunlap-Shohl et al. (2024) combined physicochemical information with machine learning to investigate T80 prediction from 45 degradation experiments on CH₃NH₃PbI₃-based cells. This work is an important precedent because it explicitly treats early ageing as information from which a future performance threshold might be estimated. Its scope nevertheless underlines the conditional nature of such predictions: performance depends on the investigated device system, the available observation period, the definition of T80 and the distinction between observations already containing a threshold event and genuinely prospective forecasts. In particular, a model can fit the early curvature of a trajectory while producing a highly uncertain crossing time, especially if small parameter changes shift the extrapolated threshold substantially. Thus, degradation-curve fit and T80 forecast error must be evaluated separately rather than treating one as evidence for the other.

Taken together, these investigations reveal a progression from large-scale performance characterization to trajectory analysis, thermally accelerated kinetics, materials-specific stability improvement and model-assisted lifetime estimation. They do not establish one universally applicable degradation law, nor do they imply that statistical model flexibility necessarily improves future-lifetime prediction. The key unresolved issue for the selected experimental archives is **how the kinetic model, temperature range, observation window and experimental sampling structure influence both estimated degradation rates and the reliability of subsequent T80 predictions**. This question is not resolved by reporting an Arrhenius coefficient or a low curve-fitting error alone. It requires rates defined on comparable time intervals, attention to sample and pixel dependence, and predictions evaluated against threshold events not used to construct them.

The present investigation is therefore positioned as a critical evaluation of temperature-dependent degradation kinetics and lifetime prediction, rather than a claim that Arrhenius modeling or T80 forecasting is new. Two public accelerated-ageing datasets provide time-resolved MPPT efficiency measurements across nominal temperatures of 25–65 °C. Their differing observation periods and device contexts are treated as features of the experimental evidence, not ignored by pooling all readings into one interchangeable population. Linear, exponential and stretched-exponential descriptions can be evaluated on common time intervals, while apparent Arrhenius parameters are interpreted within the rate definitions and temperature groups that generated them. T80 analysis additionally distinguishes an observed sustained decline from an extrapolated crossing, an event already present before the forecast origin, and a device for which the endpoint is not observed. Such distinctions are consequential for photovoltaic reliability: **a model may offer a useful description of how a cell has degraded without providing dependable evidence of how long the cell will continue to operate above a defined performance threshold**. Where independent validation fails to support reliable T80 prediction, that limitation is itself an evidence-based outcome of the investigation.

## Interconnected limitations emerging from the evidence

| Evidence-based issue | Specific precedent | Scientific implication |
|---|---|---|
| Variable degradation patterns | Hartono et al. (2023), 2,245 early-ageing curves | A single curve shape should not be assumed before model fitting; early trajectory clusters are not mechanistic labels. |
| Sensitivity of apparent kinetics to the measured endpoint and ageing phase | Flexible MAPbI₃ module study (2024), 85–105 °C | Specify and test the rate definition, temperature range and observation window before comparing apparent activation energies. |
| Device architecture as a source of heterogeneity | Alsulami et al. (2024); interfacial bilayer study (2025 volume) | Separate device systems and account for sample hierarchy rather than pooling every measurement as independent evidence. |
| Prediction objective and validation design | Alsulami et al. (2024) versus Dunlap-Shohl et al. (2024) | Prediction of observed PCE is not equivalent to prospectively predicting the future T80 threshold; evaluate each with its own test design. |
| Restricted observation horizons | Hartono et al. (2023), 150-h curves; lifetime prediction studies | Unobserved lifetime events and extrapolated crossing times require explicit censoring and uncertainty treatment. |

## Focused unresolved research question

**To what extent do temperature-dependent kinetic models provide consistent descriptions of perovskite solar-cell degradation and reliable predictions of T80 when evaluated across distinct temperatures, observation windows and independent experimental samples?**

This question is scoped to the selected experimental systems; it is not a claim that similar problems have never been studied.

## Data sources and interpretation boundaries

The project's temperature-resolved MPPT datasets are available at [Zenodo 10902908](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.10902908) and [Zenodo 21242242](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21242242). The complementary 2,245-curve Hartono dataset is at [Zenodo 8185883](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.8185883), with [original analysis code](https://github.com/noortitan/PvkSOM). The public processed 150-h pickle lacks the complete per-curve temperature and device-identity mapping; it must not be silently treated as temperature-resolved. The two accelerated-ageing archives have different follow-up durations and should not be indiscriminately merged.

**Scientific boundary:** The previously obtained project validation did **not** establish robust general superiority of a stretched-exponential model or reliable prospective T80 estimates. Do not convert curve-fitting performance or descriptive T80 classifications into a claim of validated service lifetime. Apparent activation energies are model- and endpoint-dependent, not evidence on their own for one microscopic mechanism.
